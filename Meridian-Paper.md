# MERIDIAN: Historical Architecture as Active Material — A Study in Disciplined Transformation

**Author:** Parham Mirzaei Sanatgar
**Institution:** Tehran Azad University
**Document type:** Design research paper (Dossier III of the MERIDIAN project)
**Status:** Draft v0.1
**Related materials:** [Dossier I — Research](../01_RESEARCH/), [Dossier II — Design/Construction](../02_DESIGN/), project repository README

---

## Abstract

Architecture students working with historical sources are usually offered two available positions: reproduce a style faithfully, or abandon historical reference altogether in favor of an ungrounded contemporary language. This paper documents a third position, developed and tested through an undergraduate design project — MERIDIAN — that treats four historical architectural territories (the Byzantine/Renaissance tradition represented by Saint Peter's Basilica, the Russian tradition represented by Saint Basil's Cathedral, Ilkhanid Iranian architecture represented by the Mausoleum of Oljaytu at Soltaniyeh and the mosque at Varamin, and Achaemenid Iranian architecture represented by the Apadana at Persepolis) not as styles to be quoted but as evidence bases from which structural, spatial, and proportional operators can be extracted and recombined into a new architectural proposition. The paper reports the research process behind this transformation, distinguishes historical fact from design invention throughout — most consequentially in showing that the project's dome, column, and courtyard counts are the author's own design decisions rather than citations of any source — and argues that Ilkhanid Iranian architecture's documented priority in double-shell dome construction repositions Iranian identity in the project from ornamental reference to structural genealogy. The paper further reports on the project's evidentiary discipline (a graded distinction between established fact, scholarly interpretation, and design decision) as a transferable contribution in its own right, independent of the specific building it produced. The paper closes by naming, in the author's own words, where this argument is weakest and what would be required to strengthen it.

**Keywords:** architectural transformation, critical regionalism, Ilkhanid architecture, design research methodology, evidence-based design, historical reinterpretation, Iranian architecture, research through design

## 1. Introduction

A recurring failure mode in architecture education, when a studio brief asks for a historically inspired building, is that the assignment gets answered twice over — once by students who reproduce a historical style competently and once by students who abandon history for an ungrounded contemporary gesture — without a third option ever being seriously attempted: treating historical architecture as active material for producing something that belongs to neither its sources nor to a stylistic vacuum. This paper documents an attempt at that third option.

MERIDIAN began as a response to a specific, ordinary constraint: a third-semester studio brief requiring a detail-rich, historically inspired building on a single hand-rendered 100 × 70 cm sheet, under a compressed timeline, from a student with no prior experience producing an architectural presentation drawing at this scale. The obvious response — select a historical style and render it competently — was rejected on the stated basis that copying history is not the same as understanding it. What followed was an attempt to develop, largely without formal instruction in design research methodology, a working answer to a harder question: what does it actually mean to transform historical architecture rather than reproduce or combine it?

This paper is not a defense of the finished building. It is a report on the research and reasoning that produced it, written with the same evidentiary discipline the project claims to value: historical fact is distinguished from interpretation, and interpretation is distinguished from design decision, throughout. Where the project's own numbers — a dome count, a column count — turn out not to derive from any historical source, this paper says so plainly, because a design decision honestly labeled as such is not a weakness in a project like this one; a design decision quietly presented as historical derivation would be.

## 2. Research Problem

Stated as a problem rather than a design brief, MERIDIAN addresses five nested difficulties, moving from the most abstract to the most concrete.

At the broadest level is the question of what it means to transform history into architecture, as distinct from reproducing it or merely combining several historical references side by side. Beneath that sits an architectural problem: which spatial, structural, and formal logics can actually be extracted from a historical source without carrying that source's visual identity along with them, and which cannot survive the transfer. Beneath that is a cultural problem specific to this project's material: how Iranian architectural identity can enter a hybrid, contemporary building as structural logic rather than as surface quotation — the difference between architecture that is Iranian in its bones and architecture that is Iranian in its tile pattern. Beneath that sit formal and spatial problems: how extracted, cross-territory logics generate one coherent new form rather than four recognizable quotations placed adjacently, and how the resulting transformation changes how a body actually moves through and experiences the building, not only how the building looks in plan or elevation. At the most concrete level sit technical and representational problems: which of the resulting architectural gestures are structurally plausible rather than purely geometric, and how the whole argument can be shown — on one physical sheet, and in a public research repository — without collapsing into either a historical-reference collage or an abstract, illustration-free diagram.

These five problem levels are not independent. A design decision that resolves the representational problem (something that looks finished) without having resolved the architectural and cultural problems first (something that is actually extracted and transformed rather than borrowed) has not solved the project's stated problem — it has evaded it. This paper's methodology section describes the discipline adopted specifically to prevent that evasion.

## 3. Theoretical Framework

Two existing bodies of theory frame this project, and this paper is explicit that both are frames the author found and adopted after significant independent development had already occurred, not frameworks the project was designed from at the outset. That sequence — practice first, theoretical location second — is itself reported honestly here rather than smoothed into a more conventional-sounding "framework-then-design" narrative, because the actual sequence matters for evaluating the project's originality claims (see Section 12, Counter-arguments).

**Critical regionalism.** Kenneth Frampton's *Towards a Critical Regionalism: Six Points for an Architecture of Resistance* (1983), building on a term coined earlier by Alexander Tzonis and Liane Lefaivre (1981), argues for an architecture that mediates between global modernity and local particularity by engaging topography, climate, light, tectonics, and the resistance of local building culture, rather than by applying regional motifs as surface ornament to an otherwise placeless building. MERIDIAN's stated ambition — Iranian identity as structural logic rather than applied ornament — is a direct instance of the distinction critical regionalism draws, though the project was not consciously developed as an application of Frampton's framework; the alignment was recognized after the fact, during the research phase documented in this paper.

**Research through design.** Christopher Frayling's *Research in Art and Design* (1993) distinguishes research *into* art and design (historical, aesthetic, or theoretical study of existing work), research *for* art and design (research whose outcome is an artefact), and research *through* art and design (where the act of making is itself the research method, generating knowledge that could not have been reached by other means). MERIDIAN sits primarily in the third category: the double-shell dome logic, the courtyard geometry, and the column rhythm were not first theorized and then illustrated; they were arrived at through design decisions later checked against historical evidence, in a genuinely iterative rather than linear sequence. This paper adopts Frayling's category explicitly to avoid a common misreading of design-research projects — that unresolved practical decisions are theoretical weaknesses — when in this research mode, practice generating and testing an idea, in whatever order that actually happens, is the method, not a deviation from it.

Neither framework was adequate on its own to describe what this project needed to do with its evidence, which is the subject of Section 7 (Methodology): a working discipline for keeping historical fact, scholarly interpretation, and the author's own design decisions from silently collapsing into one undifferentiated "research-informed" voice.

## 4. Literature Review

This review does not attempt comprehensive coverage of four architectural traditions spanning roughly two thousand years; that would not serve this paper's purpose, and it is not what MERIDIAN's own research process attempted either. It instead reviews, territory by territory, the specific evidence the project actually needed and used, and states plainly where that evidence is well established, where it is disputed, and where it is thin.

**Byzantine/Renaissance — Saint Peter's Basilica.** The dome composition of Saint Peter's is more historically contested than its silhouette suggests. Donato Bramante's 1506 plan proposed a Greek-cross church with a central dome and four subsidiary domes at the diagonal axes. Michelangelo, who later assumed direction of the project, is credited with the central dome's structure — completed after his death substantially according to his design — but left no known design for subsidiary domes. The subsidiary domes actually built at the front of the church are the work of Carlo Maderno's collaborator Giacomo della Porta; the two that would have stood at the rear were never built, because Maderno's later nave extension converted the plan from a Greek cross to a Latin cross and removed the space they would have occupied (Ackerman, 1986). The building's present dome composition is therefore the layered result of at least three architects working across roughly a century, not a single coherent authorial plan — a fact this paper's Findings section (Section 9) treats as directly relevant to MERIDIAN's own numbers.

**Russian — Saint Basil's Cathedral.** The stone cathedral standing in Moscow today comprises nine domed churches. This is not the building's original condition in either count or color. Before the stone cathedral, a wooden Trinity Church stood on the site, progressively surrounded by seven votive wooden churches marking successive victories of Ivan IV's campaign against Kazan and Astrakhan; the stone cathedral built from 1555–1561 reorganized this into eight chapels around a ninth, central chapel dedicated to the Intercession, with a tenth chapel added in 1588 over the grave of Basil, the local saint for whom the cathedral is popularly named (Wikipedia contributors, citing primary architectural-historical sources; cross-checked against Historic Mysteries, 2023, and Architectuul). The cathedral's celebrated multicolored appearance is similarly a later acquisition: the domes were originally gilded, with color added gradually between the 1680s and the building's completion of its present appearance by 1683 (Shvidkovsky, 2007). Two historically distinct buildings and two historically distinct color states are commonly compressed, in popular reference, into a single timeless image — a compression this paper is careful not to repeat.

**Ilkhanid Iranian — Soltaniyeh, Varamin, Natanz.** The Mausoleum of Oljaytu at Soltaniyeh (1302–1312 CE) is documented by UNESCO as the earliest extant double-shell dome in Iran: an inner structural shell and an outer shell, separated by an interstitial space, standing roughly 50 metres tall (UNESCO World Heritage Centre, listing 1188). Separately from this well-established priority-in-Iran claim, a specific and more contested scholarly hypothesis proposes that this double-shell technique influenced Filippo Brunelleschi's later dome for Florence Cathedral: Piero Sanpaolesi first argued the connection in 1972, and Howard Saalman's architectural history of Brunelleschi's dome (1980) independently notes that the double-shell concept was established well before Brunelleschi and Ghiberti's involvement in the Florence project. This transmission hypothesis is not unanimous — the architectural historian André Godard disputed whether Soltaniyeh's structure constitutes a true double dome at all, against the earlier claims of Jane Dieulafoy — and this paper treats the transmission question as a live scholarly dispute rather than a settled fact, while treating Soltaniyeh's priority within Iran as well established. Beyond the dome, Ilkhanid religious architecture organizes space around a four-iwan courtyard plan, staging a threshold sequence from portal to vestibule to iwan to domed prayer hall, documented at the Congregational Mosque of Varamin (1322); and among the earliest surviving muqarnas-vaulted entrance portals in Iran is the Shrine of Shaykh 'Abd al-Samad at Natanz (1307).

**Achaemenid Iranian — Persepolis.** The Apadana at Persepolis, begun under Darius I around 518 BCE and completed under Xerxes I, was a hypostyle hall of 72 columns, each approximately 24 metres tall, of which 14 remain standing (Schmidt, 1953; Smarthistory). The nearby Throne Hall, the so-called Hall of a Hundred Columns, was begun under Xerxes and completed under Artaxerxes I at roughly 68.5 by 68.5 metres (Livius; Persian Column, Wikipedia). Approach to the terrace was staged through the Gate of All Nations and the Tripylon before reaching the Apadana's own monumental stairways, whose relief carvings depict processions of tribute-bearing subject peoples — read by art historians as an idealized image of imperial order, an interpretation rather than a documented statement of intent.

### 4.1 Positioning within existing frameworks

A project developed largely outside formal architectural-theory coursework runs a specific risk: independently arriving at a working vocabulary — "transformation," "extraction," "emergence" — that overlaps substantially with existing, named frameworks the author had not yet encountered, and presenting that vocabulary as more original than it is. This paper states plainly where that overlap exists. MERIDIAN's central ambition (identity through structural logic rather than ornament) is not a new claim; it is Frampton's critical-regionalist argument, arrived at independently and only later located within Frampton's own terms. Its research mode (design decisions generating knowledge, later checked against evidence) is not a new claim either; it is close to what Frayling calls research through design. What this paper argues MERIDIAN contributes is not a new theoretical position but a specific, documented case — an undergraduate design process, unusually transparent about its own evidentiary status — applying these existing positions to a four-territory transformation problem that, as far as this review has established, has not been attempted in quite this combination before.

## 5. Research Gaps

Three gaps, identified during the research process itself rather than after the fact, materially affect the strength of this paper's claims and are carried forward into the Discussion and Limitations sections rather than resolved by assertion.

First, the structural logic behind the roughly 185 columns proposed along the project's courtyard walls has not been derived from a stated module, rhythm, or proportional rule; if such a derivation exists, it has not yet been written down, and if it does not exist, that should be stated as plainly as the numbers that are openly design decisions (Section 9). Second, the project's kinetic dome mechanism — intended to modulate daylight through the stepped dome levels — has no identified architectural precedent at the scale and integration this project proposes, and its mode of actuation remains undefined beyond the level of concept. Third, no specific historical building among the sources reviewed demonstrates a threshold principle recurring at more than one physical scale within a single structure; MERIDIAN's claim that its own threshold logic recurs at multiple scales is, at present, the author's interpretive extension of the Ilkhanid four-iwan principle rather than a historically documented pattern.

## 6. Research Question

The research problem in Section 2 and the gaps identified through the literature reviewed in Section 4 converge on one central, answerable question:

> How can historical architectural knowledge — specifically, the structural, spatial, and proportional logics documented across the Byzantine/Renaissance, Russian, Ilkhanid Iranian, and Achaemenid Iranian traditions — be extracted and transformed into a coherent new architectural language, in a way that is traceable to its sources, honest about what in the result is derived versus decided, and specifically capable of repositioning Iranian architectural identity as structural rather than ornamental?

Three subordinate questions structure the Findings and Discussion that follow: which specific operators, not surface features, can be responsibly extracted from each source (Section 9); where the four sources' logics compete rather than align, and how that competition was resolved (Section 10); and whether the resulting building's own numerical and geometric decisions can be defended on their own terms once they are shown not to be historical citations (Sections 9–11).

## 7. Methodology

MERIDIAN's design method proceeds through a fixed sequence — observation, decomposition, extraction, transformation, spatial and formal synthesis, emergence — applied to each of the four source territories independently before cross-comparison. What distinguishes the method reported here from an unstructured "inspired by" design process is a single working discipline, developed partway through the project once its own early collage-like drafts revealed the need for it: every claim made about a historical source, and every number or form decided by the project itself, is graded into one of a small number of categories before it is allowed into the design record.

The grading scale distinguishes an established fact (documented with minimal interpretive distance — for instance, that Saint Basil's stone cathedral has nine domes), a scholarly consensus (documented but resting on some interpretive framing — the four-iwan plan's threshold function), a plausible scholarly interpretation (a reasonable but non-unanimous reading of evidence — the Apadana reliefs as an idealized image of imperial order), the author's own interpretation (clearly the project's voice, not the source's — a threshold principle "recurring" across scales), a design decision belonging to the project rather than any source (the seven-dome count, the five Achaemenid-derived columns), a working hypothesis with a stated path to resolution (the zigguratic dome's structural plausibility), an explicitly experimental proposition (the kinetic mechanism as an implementable system), and an item that remains genuinely unresolved.

This grading was applied retroactively to material generated earlier in the project as well as prospectively to material generated after its adoption — a limitation reported honestly in Section 13, since retroactive grading risks reconstructing a decision's justification after the fact rather than recovering its actual original basis. Where the author could not confidently reconstruct whether an early decision derived from a source or from unrecorded design intuition, this paper labels it a design decision by default, on the reasoning that unearned historical authority is the more serious error to avoid, even at the cost of somewhat underclaiming the project's engagement with its own sources in a small number of cases.
## 8. Physical Artifact and Case Study

### 8.1 The primary artifact: the MERIDIAN sheet

The project's primary artifact is a hand-produced architectural presentation sheet, 100 × 70 cm, mounted on a wooden frame roughly 5 cm deep and backlit by four LEDs so that layered drawings and diagrams are revealed differently under direct and backlit viewing — a physical instance, on the sheet itself, of the light/shadow logic the proposed building's kinetic dome mechanism is meant to enact at architectural scale. The building it depicts organizes seven stepped, ziggurat-profiled domes — one large central dome and six smaller ones, each built up in three tiers — around a base plan that preserves Saint Peter's cruciform organization without alteration, deliberately worked within as a constraint rather than redesigned. A courtyard of two curving, convergent arms, stepping up in height as they approach the main building, is lined with roughly 185 columns in a column-arch-column rhythm drawing on Ilkhanid small-arch proportion, and includes semi-hidden corridors within the courtyard walls providing upper-floor access. Of the six smaller domes, five carry Saint Basil-derived pattern logic and two carry Ilkhanid-derived pattern logic — an explicit, visible record, on the building itself, of the four-source division of labor argued for in Section 10. The design deliberately excludes crosses, lanterns, spires, onion forms, and other religious iconography from the dome profiles, on the reasoning that the transformation should be legible as architectural logic rather than as any one tradition's devotional symbolism.

The sheet was submitted to its originating studio in an unfinished state — the final rendered image was not completed within the available time — and received a full grade; a faculty member subsequently proposed the work for permanent framed display at the university. This paper does not treat that outcome as validation of the argument made here; a studio grade evaluates different things than a research claim, and this paper's Limitations section (13) says so explicitly.

### 8.2 A second case study, pending

A methodological weakness of relying on a single artifact — even one documented as thoroughly as the sheet above — is that it cannot, by itself, demonstrate that MERIDIAN's method is a repeatable procedure rather than a description written after the fact to fit one already-finished design. The strongest available answer to that weakness is a second, independent worked example: a smaller design problem, carried through the same observation–extraction–transformation–synthesis–emergence sequence, with its own extraction table and its own honestly graded numbers, produced separately from the original sheet and evaluable on its own.

This section is intentionally left as a defined placeholder rather than filled with an invented example, because a fabricated "sample system output" would defeat the exact evidentiary discipline this paper argues for. Two paths would complete it: applying the method, in view, to a second architectural problem chosen for this purpose; or documenting a genuine secondary output already produced during the project's development, if one exists, with the same evidentiary grading used throughout this paper. Which path to take is a decision for the next revision of this paper, not one this draft makes on the author's behalf.

## 9. Findings

Three findings emerged from the research process and materially shape this paper's argument.

**Finding 1 — none of the four source territories offers a clean historical precedent for MERIDIAN's own numbers.** Saint Peter's dome composition, as established in Section 4, was never a single architect's unified six-subsidiary-dome plan; Bramante proposed four, della Porta built two, and two were never built. Saint Basil's has nine domes today, descended from an earlier arrangement of seven wooden votive churches belonging to a different, predecessor building. The Apadana had 72 columns and the Throne Hall roughly 100; MERIDIAN's five Achaemenid-derived columns are a substantial reduction with no numerical precedent in the source. The Ilkhanid four-iwan courtyard has four arms, not the two convergent arms MERIDIAN proposes. In every case checked, the project's specific number is a design decision, not a citation — a conclusion this paper states without treating it as a defect, provided it is stated rather than obscured (Section 7).

**Finding 2 — Ilkhanid Iranian architecture holds a documented, if partially contested, priority claim over the Renaissance in double-shell dome construction.** Soltaniyeh's dome (1302–1312) is UNESCO-documented as the earliest such structure in Iran, predating Michelangelo's dome for Saint Peter's (design credited from 1547) by well over two centuries and predating Brunelleschi's dome for Florence Cathedral by roughly a century. Whether construction knowledge specifically transmitted from Ilkhanid Iran to Renaissance Italy remains a genuine, unresolved scholarly dispute — advanced by Sanpaolesi (1972) and supported by Saalman's (1980) independent observation that the double-shell concept predates Brunelleschi's involvement, but not unanimous, given André Godard's earlier dissent about whether Soltaniyeh constitutes a true double dome at all. This paper treats the priority claim as well supported and the transmission claim as contested, and argues in Section 11 that this distinction is sufficient, on its own, to reposition Iranian architecture's role in the project.

**Finding 3 — the four source territories were not asked to solve the same design problem.** Cross-checking the extraction record (documented in full in Dossier I) shows Saint Peter's contributing scale and axial hierarchy, Saint Basil's contributing dome multiplicity with internal differentiation, Ilkhanid architecture contributing structural depth and threshold sequence, and Achaemenid architecture contributing column rhythm and processional pacing — four distinct architectural roles, not four decorative quotations layered onto one form. This division of labor is treated in Section 10 as the project's primary evidence against the collage objection that a four-source project of this kind would otherwise invite.

## 10. Analyses

Two points of direct conflict between the source territories surfaced during research, and both are treated here as productive rather than as inconsistencies to conceal.

The double-shell dome principle could plausibly be credited to either Saint Peter's or Soltaniyeh; citing only Saint Peter's would silently repeat a common historiographical default in which structural innovation is assumed to originate in Renaissance Europe. Given Finding 2, this paper's position is that Soltaniyeh is the primary reference for the operator MERIDIAN calls layered enclosure — an inner structural shell, an outer shell, and a load-distributing interstitial space — with Saint Peter's treated as a later, comparative instance of the same underlying principle appearing independently, or possibly derivatively, elsewhere. This is not a claim that Brunelleschi or Michelangelo copied Soltaniyeh; it is a claim about which structure demonstrates the principle first, which is a narrower and better-supported claim.

The second conflict concerns courtyard geometry. The Ilkhanid four-iwan tradition organizes a courtyard with four arms; the Achaemenid processional sequence is linear and single-axis, not courtyard-organized at all. MERIDIAN's courtyard, generated by two curved, convergent arms, is a synthesis of both — drawing threshold-and-compression logic from the four-iwan tradition and controlled, staged approach from the Achaemenid sequence — rather than a citation of either. This analysis treats that synthesis as the clearest single demonstration, in the whole project, of transformation actually occurring rather than being claimed: no available source, on its own, would have produced this specific geometry.

Read together with Finding 1, these two analyses support a more general claim about the shape of MERIDIAN's originality. The project's contribution is not in inventing numbers that sound authoritative; it is in the specific, checkable work of deciding which source contributes which operator, and in the one clear case (the courtyard) where two operators from different sources had to be reconciled into a form neither source contains.

## 11. Discussion

### 11.1 What repositioning Iranian identity actually changes

Section 9's second finding does more analytical work than it might first appear to. At the project's origin, Iranian architectural identity was expected to enter mainly through the building's entrance sequence — deep thresholds, muqarnas-derived ornament, turquoise and gold — a valid contribution, but one confined to the building's edge and its most visible, most easily dismissed-as-decorative surfaces. Locating the double-shell dome's primary reference in Soltaniyeh instead moves Iranian architecture's contribution to the least visible, least decorative part of the building: its structural section. A hybrid building's most "Iranian" feature, on this account, is not the courtyard's ornament but the load path inside its central dome — precisely the part of a building least reducible to a decorative sticker, which is the specific failure mode critical regionalism (Section 3) warns against.

This does not resolve the identity question entirely. Sections 8's entrance-level ornament remains part of the design, and remains the most visually legible layer of Iranian reference in the finished building; it is real, but it is not now asked to carry the argument alone.

### 11.2 What this approach makes newly possible

Two things become available once a project adopts this paper's evidentiary discipline that are not available to a project that does not. First, a design decision can be defended honestly on its own architectural merits — why five columns rather than seventy-two changes the reading of a facade from a field of columns to a small number of individually legible monumental elements — without needing to borrow unearned historical authority to justify it. Second, a genuinely valuable historiographical point (Finding 2) becomes visible and usable precisely because the method required checking every claim against real evidence rather than assuming the more commonly told version of dome history. Neither of these outcomes is available to a project willing to let "inspired by history" remain permanently vague.

### 11.3 Implications beyond this project

If Ilkhanid Iranian architecture's structural contributions are more often subordinated to its ornamental reputation in general architectural-historical discourse than the underlying evidence supports — a pattern this paper's research process encountered directly, though a systematic claim about the discourse as a whole is beyond what this paper can establish — then a design-research method that requires checking structural priority before assuming it, as practiced here, may have value for other projects working with non-Western architectural sources under similar pressure to default to the more familiar, Eurocentric account.

## 12. Counter-arguments

Three objections to this paper's argument are anticipated here directly, in the author's own voice, rather than left for others to raise unaddressed.

**The "relabeled arbitrariness" objection.** One could argue that calling an unexplained number a "design decision" rather than an "error" is merely a more sophisticated way of avoiding the plain fact that the number is arbitrary — that the D2 label in this paper's grading scale functions as absolution rather than honesty. This paper's response: a design decision is not required to derive from an external source to be defensible; it is required to be defensible on its own architectural terms, and to be labeled honestly rather than dressed as citation. Not every number in this paper meets that bar yet — the 185-column count, named as an open gap in Section 5, does not yet have a stated derivation and should not be read as more resolved than it is. Where a number does have a stated architectural rationale (the five-column reduction, discussed in Section 11.2), the label is doing real work; where it does not yet, this paper says so rather than filling the gap with a plausible-sounding justification invented after the fact.

**The "sophisticated collage" objection.** One could argue that assigning each of four sources a distinct role is simply collage with a more disciplined vocabulary attached, since the finished building still contains four legible historical citations. This paper's response, developed in Section 10, is that the courtyard geometry is the specific counter-example: it is not a citation of any source, but a synthesis that required reconciling two conflicting geometries. That single case does not prove the whole building escapes collage, and this paper does not claim it does; the dome system and the column reduction still await comparably specific synthesis arguments, which is why this remains a live rather than fully settled objection.

**The "overclaimed priority" objection.** One could argue that Finding 2 overstates a contested Iran-to-Italy transmission theory to elevate the project's own cultural framing. This paper's response is that Sections 4, 9, and 10 are explicit that the transmission claim is contested and that only the narrower priority-within-Iran claim, which is UNESCO-documented and essentially uncontested, is treated as established. If a reader encounters a version of this argument elsewhere in the MERIDIAN materials that blurs this distinction, that is an error in that material, not a defensible reading of the evidence, and should be corrected against this paper's more careful statement of the claim.

## 13. Strengths and Limitations

Rather than a separate ledger of virtues, this paper's strengths are stated where they belong — in Section 11's account of what the evidentiary discipline makes possible, and in Section 9's findings, which stand or fall on their own evidence rather than on being asserted as strengths. What follows is a direct account of where this research method cannot go.

Historical and art-historical research of the kind conducted here can establish that a building exists, when it was built, and what scholars have argued about it; it cannot recover a historical patron's or builder's actual intent, and readings offered as interpretation throughout this paper (the Apadana reliefs' symbolism, threshold sequences read as spiritual staging) should not be mistaken for recovered intent. The Soltaniyeh–Florence transmission question is a genuine, unresolved historiographical dispute that this research method can report but cannot resolve. No structural engineering analysis has been performed on MERIDIAN's proposed zigguratic dome or kinetic mechanism; historical comparison to Soltaniyeh's engineering establishes plausibility by analogy, not buildability, and claims about either should be read at the hypothesis grade assigned to them in Dossier I, not as settled findings. No empirical study of reader or viewer experience has been conducted; claims in this project about cognitive engagement or "human connection" are, at present, design intentions rather than demonstrated effects. Finally, because this project's theoretical framing (Section 3) was assembled after most of the design work was already underway, some genuine overlap with existing literature the author had not yet encountered at the time of design almost certainly exists beyond what Section 4.1 has so far identified; this paper's positioning claims should be treated as a first pass, not a completed literature search.

## 14. Unanswered Questions

Beyond the limitations above, four questions remain open and are not expected to be resolved by this version of the paper: what specific derivation, if any, underlies the 185-column count; what an actual, evaluable mechanical concept for the kinetic dome mechanism would look like, sufficient for a structural or mechanical engineer to assess; whether a trained architectural critic encountering this project without its supporting research materials would recognize the four-source division of labor argued for in Section 10, or would instead read the building as collage regardless of the reasoning behind it; and what a second, independent case study (Section 8.2) would reveal about whether this method transfers to a design problem other than the one it was developed to solve.

## 15. Contribution

This paper's contribution is threefold. First, it offers a specific historiographical correction of modest but real scope: within the context of this project's own argument, Ilkhanid Iranian architecture's double-shell dome construction has a better-documented priority claim than the more commonly assumed Renaissance-originated account, and Section 11 shows what changes in a design project's identity claims once that correction is taken seriously. Second, it documents a transferable evidentiary method — the fact/interpretation/decision/hypothesis grading scale used throughout this paper — that other design-research projects working with historical sources could adopt independently of MERIDIAN's specific architectural content. Third, and more modestly, it documents a case: an undergraduate design process that, without formal training in design research methodology, arrived independently at practices — evidentiary grading, honest labeling of design decisions, explicit theoretical positioning after the fact — that align with existing, established scholarly practice in the field. That convergence is offered here as evidence for those practices' value, not as a claim of priority over the scholars who established them first.

## 16. Conclusion

MERIDIAN set out to answer a question with no comfortable default response: whether historical architecture can be transformed rather than reproduced or combined, in a way that is honest about the difference. The research reported in this paper does not conclude that MERIDIAN has fully succeeded at that; Sections 12 through 14 name real, unresolved objections and open questions, and this paper considers that naming to be part of the project's contribution rather than a concession that undermines it. What the research does establish is narrower and more defensible: that the project's own numbers are design decisions rather than historical citations, and are stronger for being labeled as such; that one specific historiographical repositioning — Iranian architecture's structural rather than ornamental role — is supported by real, if partially contested, evidence; and that at least one part of the design, the courtyard geometry, demonstrates genuine synthesis rather than juxtaposition. Whether the completed building, evaluated as architecture rather than as argument, earns the term its author hopes it might one day deserve is a question this paper leaves open, deliberately, for the reader and for future revisions of this document to continue testing.
