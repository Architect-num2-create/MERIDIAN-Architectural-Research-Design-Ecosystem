# 11 — SPATIAL GRAMMAR

Spatial grammar is where MERIDIAN stops being a collection of formal moves and starts becoming architecture.

The question is not "what does the plan look like?"
It is "what kind of spatial event does each transformation create?"

---

## The project seems to want a centre — but the centre is not automatically the middle.

A centre becomes architectural when other spaces have a reason to relate to it.

It can collect movement.
It can receive light.
It can hold the largest volume.
It can become the moment where several geometries meet.
It can also be deliberately difficult to reach.

So the real question is:

**What makes this centre necessary?**

Until that is answered, "centrality" is only geometry.

---

## The same thing applies to the edge.

The peripheral masses should not simply surround the centre because that makes a convincing image.

They need their own condition.

They might:
- compress the centre,
- protect it,
- frame it,
- open toward it,
- turn away from it,
- create smaller rooms around it,
- create a changing distance from it.

This is where multiplicity becomes spatial rather than visual.

---

## Movement

The project already contains transformation through splitting, aggregation, radial movement and sectional exposure.

That should eventually be felt by the body.

A person should not only see that the building has been transformed.
They should **move through the transformation**.

Maybe:

compressed → released  
low → high  
dark → light  
straight → turning  
single → multiple  
outside → threshold → inner field → centre

Not all of these need to happen.
But if the geometry changes dramatically while the experience stays generic, something is missing.

---

## Threshold

The entrance should not be treated as a hole in the outer wall.

It can be the first transformation.

Outside geometry might be relatively controlled.
The threshold begins to break it.
The interior reveals another order.

This could be one of the places where Iranian architectural intelligence becomes spatial rather than decorative:
not by copying a historical entrance, but by making **depth itself do architectural work**.

---

## Void

The voids in this project cannot be leftovers.

A void can:
- separate masses,
- connect them,
- bring light deep into the building,
- establish hierarchy,
- create a second centre,
- make a thick wall readable,
- control views,
- slow movement.

The interesting question is not "where are the courtyards?"

It is:

**What changes because the void exists?**

---

## A useful test

Take one plan and remove the historical references from your mind.

Walk it.

Ask:

Where do I feel the building becoming something else?
Where does the scale change?
Where does the centre become apparent?
Where do I lose orientation?
Where do I want to move?
Where does light pull me?
Where does the building hold me back?

Those moments are more important than the diagram that produced them.

---

## Still unresolved

- Is the centre a room, a volume, a courtyard, a vertical event, or a combination?
- Is the main movement axial, radial, or deliberately caught between the two?
- Does the split create two worlds, or does it create a more complex unity?
- How much of the transformation should be visible to the visitor?
- Where does the building become unexpectedly intimate?

These should be discovered through plans and sections, not answered by writing.
