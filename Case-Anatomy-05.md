# Case Anatomy of MERIDIAN

Strip away the defense-ready language and here is what the case actually is.

**The object.** A hand-built presentation board, roughly 100 by 70 centimeters, mounted on a wooden frame with about five centimeters of depth behind it, lit from within by four LEDs. The light is not decorative — it is structural to the reading of the piece. Layers that are invisible under ambient light appear when it is backlit, meaning the board has two states, and most viewers will only ever see one of them.

**The plan.** A cruciform footprint borrowed intact from Saint Peter's Basilica and deliberately never altered — the one source the project agreed to work *within* rather than *against*. Onto that plan sit seven stepped, ziggurat-profile domes: one large central dome and six smaller ones, each built in three tiers. Five of the six small domes carry Saint Basil's pattern-and-rhythm logic; the remaining two carry Ilkhanid ornament instead. None of the seven carries a cross, a lantern, a spire, or an onion profile — the single most consistent editorial decision in the entire project, and arguably the real thesis statement, since it is the one place every source was equally overruled.

**The threshold.** Ilkhanid deep-entrance geometry, arches, ornament, turquoise and gold — the project's most legible "skin," and for that reason its most vulnerable to being read as decoration rather than logic.

**The colonnade.** Roughly 185 columns ring the courtyard in a column–arch–column rhythm, small Ilkhanid arches between them. Inside the main volume, five structural columns quote Achaemenid Persepolis directly — the smallest numerical footprint of any of the four sources, carrying a structural role disproportionate to its visual presence.

**The courtyard.** Two curved arms, read as a yin-yang or convergence gesture, stepping upward in height as they approach the main building — a rare moment where the geometry itself performs the project's central verb, *transformation*, rather than illustrating it. Hidden corridors run inside the courtyard walls, giving upper-floor access without a visible stair — the project's first instance of a legible surface concealing an operational one, a pattern that recurs throughout the ecosystem layers.

**The mechanism.** An "Aperture Core" — originally conceived as rings connected to a rotating central column, channeling natural light down into the building. Of everything in the case, this is the piece explicitly asked to work as *cross-era* proof: plausible if built by hand in an older era, plausible if built by machine in a future one. It is not meant to resolve into one literal spec. It is meant to survive being asked "how" by both a historian and an engineer.

**The origin wound.** Ten days. A studio assignment announced late, calling for a historically dense, ornament-heavy hand-drawn sheet. A professor who quietly grouped some students toward a simpler artistic-copy path with no engineering integration underneath it. A refusal to take that path, with no prior experience producing an architecture sheet of any kind. The concept did not arrive from a fully formed plan — it arrived from forcing two incompatible logics together and calling the friction *DNA extraction*: Saint Basil supplying the ornamental layer, Saint Peter supplying the plan and engineering complexity, the ziggurat-dome idea surfacing somewhere in that collision rather than being designed toward on purpose.

**The unresolved fact at the center of the case.** The sheet was submitted *unfinished* — the final image was never completed — and it received a full grade, 20 out of 20. A faculty member who heard the project explained afterward suggested it be framed under glass for permanent display. This is the fact the rest of the dossier keeps circling back to: an incomplete object was judged, and judged complete. Every other organ in this dossier — the contradictions, the failure atlas, the unsolved mysteries — is in some sense a downstream consequence of that one unresolved anatomical fact.
